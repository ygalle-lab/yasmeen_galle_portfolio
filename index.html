How IPV support staff handle technology-facilitated abuse, and what gets in their way

[Describe your contributions, e.g., interview notes, coding, survey analysis]
Status
Study complete; paper submitted for peer review

The problem

Support staff are often the first responders when survivors of intimate-partner violence (IPV) face technology-facilitated abuse (TFA). Yet little was known about the challenges and risks staff face in doing this work themselves.

What we did

  • Partnered with a local IPV organization and collected 55 weekly survey responses from 12 staff over six weeks, capturing the TFA issues they saw and how confident they felt addressing them.
  • Ran 15 semi-structured interviews with staff across legal, hotline, shelter, and counseling roles to understand training, support challenges, and harms they face themselves.
  • Analyzed both datasets with thematic analysis, using MaxQDA, Qualtrics, and Excel.

What we found

  • Most staff had little or no TFA-specific training, and rarely relied on it when helping survivors.
  • Staff described a three-stage process of diagnosis, intervention, and follow-up, with distinct obstacles at each stage and an emotional toll when they could not solve a problem.
  • Staff were least confident about excessive contact, hacking, and surveillance cases.
  • Staff and their organizations also face harms themselves, such as harassment and attacks on the organization.

Need finding and co-design for tools that protect people from AI image abuse

Role
Research Intern, supporting need finding and co-design
Where
Carnegie Mellon University, School of Computer Science
Status
Current work, in progress
Methods
Systematic audit, co-design

The problem

AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery is one of the most urgent privacy harms of the generative-AI era. Researchers have built technical tools that help people protect their photos from AI manipulation, but survivors have largely not benefited from them.

The gap

This research has mostly focused on making the technology more effective. Early results from an ongoing audit suggest that nearly none of these protective tools have a graphical interface, and that those that do were neither co-designed with affected communities nor usability tested with non-expert users.

What the team is doing

  • Need finding: a systematic audit of existing anti-manipulation tools.
  • Co-design: eliciting needs and design goals with education facilitators, survivors, and general users.
  • Development and evaluation: a later phase to build and test an app for protecting photos on a person's own device.

My role is to support need finding and co-design.

Testing an app that helps young adults adopt cybersecurity habits

Role
Research assistant
Where
Carnegie Mellon University, School of Computer Science
Timeline
May 2026 to present
Methods
Interviews, survey, comparison of two study conditions

The problem

Cybersecurity habits are hard to build. This study tests an app with a 21-day challenge that helps young adults make cybersecurity part of their routine, and encourages them to see these practices as part of being an adult.

What I did

  • Conducted interviews and took detailed notes on how participants used the app.
  • Documented participant feedback on usability, features, and ease of use.
  • Cleaned and analyzed interview transcripts and survey data to find barriers to adoption.
  • Support a comparison of two groups: participants who do only the cybersecurity challenge, and participants who do cybersecurity plus other chores and adulting tasks.

Where it stands

This study is a work in progress, with interviews and a survey still underway.
